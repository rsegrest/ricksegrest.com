## Title: Malaysia: My Surprising Sanctuary

The flight from Atlanta to Kuala Lumpur was brutal. Twenty-two hours with a layover in Abu Dhabi, and I lost thirteen hours along the way. Luckily I brought an inflatable airplane pillow I'd ordered from Amazon before I left, which literally saved my butt. Still, I was almost useless when I got there.

Most people don't know or think much about Malaysia. I had only recently been learning about it as a haven for digital nomads and expatriates, and I wanted to see for myself. So I went in September 2025 for the Nomad Capitalist Live event. I really didn't know what to expect, but was surprised and amazed by what I'd found. Now I want to go back, possibly to stay.

[PHOTO: majestic-hotel-lobby.jpg]
Caption: The Majestic Hotel bar, built in 1932, still has a live grand piano performance.

The Nomad Capitalist Live conference was held in the Majestic Hotel, built in 1932 during the British Colonial period. The rooms there are $110 a night. For the price of a Motel 6 in the US, I got a king bed and a standalone bathtub in addition to the walk-in shower. Breakfast and lunch were included. Not a "Continental Breakfast" -- it was a huge buffet with all kinds of things, and no good reason to leave hungry.

[PHOTO: hotel-buffet-fruit.jpg]
Caption: The Majestic Hotel breakfast buffet. If you left hungry, it was your own fault.

Food is taken very seriously in Malaysia, and like most things there, it costs a fraction of what inferior food costs here in the US. You could walk into any grocery or convenience store there and get an eight-inch cube of the best watermelon, pineapple, or any other tropical fruit for four ringgit (about one dollar). The same fruit in the US, if you could get it at all, would cost about $15, and half of the juice, texture, and flavor would have been wicked away in transit.

[PHOTO: grocery-watermelon.jpg]
Caption: Six ringgit — about $1.50 — for a huge bin of fantastic fruit.

At the nearby Central Market, you could get dinner on the street for $2-3, and it would likely rival a Michelin-rated dish.

[PHOTO: central-market-walkway.jpg]
Caption: Central Market. Dinner on the street for $2-3.

[PHOTO: japanese-ramen-dinner.jpg]
Caption: Ramen and sushi, inside food court at Lot 10 / Isetan at Bukit Bintang. The entire food court (and the rest of the mall) was authentically Japanese.

My reason for planning to attend the Nomad Capitalist event was that I've noticed that I am about ten times happier when I'm abroad. The keynote speakers included Steve Wozniak, Glenn Greenwald, former UK Chancellor of the Exchequer Kwasi Kwarteng, and MC Andrew Henderson, whose YouTube channel and book I'd been following before signing up.

The other speakers spoke in detail about how to invest, travel, and diversify away from the United States -- starting businesses and buying property abroad. I love many things about the US, but as time goes by and world dynamics shift, my wife and I have taken every opportunity to see the rest of the world. We also have dreams of a second (or first?) home base abroad.

If you're interested in Southeastern Asian culture, it all comes together here. Chinese, Japanese, Thai, and Indian people, their food, and their art and influence was juxtaposed and distributed throughout Kuala Lumpur, along with the surprisingly rich Malay culture, with remnants of British colonization. Although the slim majority of Malays practice Islam, they encourage other religious practices and welcome visitors from around the world. The Batu Caves temple (Hindu) on the edge of the city, and Chin Swee temple (Buddhist) in the nearby Genting Highlands are beautiful sanctuaries built near the city.

The city itself is more densely populated than Hong Kong, but it doesn't seem that way at all. It has grown and developed very quickly, in a vertical direction, both structurally and economically. Kuala Lumpur is a forest of modern skyscrapers rising from the jungle. Clearly following in China and Singapore's economic footsteps, not only is it highly developed, but it is also very inexpensive, safe, friendly, culturally rich, English-speaking.

[PHOTO: petronas-towers-night.jpg]
Caption: The Petronas Twin Towers at night. I didn't make it up to the skybridge. Yet.

[PHOTO: indoor-amusement-park.jpg]
Caption: An indoor amusement park in the Genting Highlands.

Our kids will be grown soon, and it doesn't take a mathematician to see that the cost of living in a place like Malaysia (or even Mexico) grants you a lot more freedom to travel, start a business, or just retire and enjoy family life if you choose.

[PHOTO: truefitt-and-hill.jpg]
Caption: Truefitt & Hill, est. 1805, St. James's London — in a Kuala Lumpur mall, where I got the best haircut of my life.

[PHOTO: majestic-hotel-robe-selfie.jpg]
Caption: Back at the Majestic, with haircut.

[PHOTO: durian-stall.jpg]
Caption: Durian Boi had to set up shop in an outdoor pavilion so he wouldn't offend anyone.

I went by myself on this trip, and I hope to take my family back there next time. I didn't get to visit the rainforest park or the bird park in the middle of the city. I couldn't get a ticket to go to the top of the Petronas Twin Towers or cross the skybridge -- 558 feet off the ground and made of glass. I didn't get to one of the many bars or restaurants at the top of a skyscraper. And I completely missed trying infamous durian, the tropical fruit native to Southeast Asia that you have to wear gloves to eat, and that's so controversial and smelly it's banned from most indoor spaces. I'm told you'll either love it or hate it, but there are very few foods that I hate.

These missed adventures give me reasons to go back. But they're not the real reason I think about Malaysia.

Unfortunately, when I got back home, my mom had major surgery scheduled. I had considered cancelling my trip. I was immediately plunged into spending most of my day confined to a hospital or physical therapy cell, trying and failing to care for my mother when she was not strong enough to recover from surgery. Trying and failing to reason with less-than-reasonable people at work who had zero sympathy for my situation, who expected me to work through a government shutdown while I was watching my mom die, as they were at home playing Dungeons & Dragons, forbidden to work.

Malaysia made me realize that there's a big world out there to explore, teeming with rich life and culture.

I felt almost guilty, at times, for going through with my plans. But I now realize that the freedom and optimism of that trip kept me from being overwhelmed with the stress, sorrow, and loss that I was confined to when I returned. I started thinking of travel not just as a great adventure, but my own form of therapy: the memories I made were a bridge back to sanity when my life buried me in burdens.
