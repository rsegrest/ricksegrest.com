## Post 1: Twenty-Two Hours to Paradise

The flight over was brutal. Twenty-two hours on the plane with a layover in Abu Dhabi, and I lost thirteen hours along the way. I'd bought an inflatable airplane pillow from Amazon before I left, which literally saved my butt, but I was pretty useless when I got there.

Most people don't know or think much about Malaysia. I didn't, either, not really. I'd been hearing about it as a haven for digital nomads and expatriates, and I wanted to see for myself. So I went in September 2025 for the Nomad Capitalist Live event.

The conference was held in the Majestic Hotel, built in 1932 during the British Colonial period. My room was $110 a night. For the price of a Motel 6 in the US, I got a king bed and a standalone bathtub in addition to the walk-in shower. Breakfast and lunch were included -- not a "Continental Breakfast," but a huge buffet with all kinds of things. If you left hungry, it was your own fault.

I've noticed something about myself: I am about ten times happier when I'm abroad. I don't know if it's the distance from everything familiar, the shock of the new, or just the feeling that the world is bigger than my daily routine. But I've been thinking about that a lot since I got back, and I'll write more about it in [part 2](#post-2-a-forest-of-skyscrapers) and [part 3](#post-3-a-bridge-back-to-sanity).

The Majestic Hotel felt like stepping into a different era. Built by the British in 1932, all colonial architecture and old-world service, but the price was anything but colonial. I kept doing the math in my head -- what this room would cost in any major US city, what the buffet alone would run you at a hotel that looked like this. The numbers didn't compare. Nothing in Malaysia compared, and that was part of the point.

I went by myself on this trip. I'd considered cancelling -- my mom had major surgery scheduled back home -- but I went. I'll get to that decision later. For now, I'll just say that the moment I stepped out of the airport into the heat and the green and the noise of Kuala Lumpur, I knew I'd made the right call, even if I didn't know why yet.

The city is more densely populated than Hong Kong, but it doesn't seem like it. It has grown vertically -- a forest of modern skyscrapers rising from the jungle. It's highly developed, very inexpensive, safe, friendly, culturally rich, English-speaking, and clearly following in China and Singapore's economic footsteps. It is a surprising and amazing place, and since I've been, I've wanted to go back. Possibly to stay.

## Post 2: A Forest of Skyscrapers

Food is taken very seriously in Malaysia, and like most things there, it costs a fraction of what inferior food costs here in the US. You could walk into any grocery or convenience store and get an eight-inch cube of the best watermelon, pineapple, or any other tropical fruit you could think of for four ringgit. About a dollar. The same fruit in the US, if you could get it at all, would cost about $15, and half the juice and flavor would have been wicked away in transit. At the nearby Central Market, you could get dinner on the street for $2-3, and it would rival any Michelin-rated dish.

If you're interested in Asian culture, you can see it all here. There are Chinese, Japanese, Thai, and Indian people, food, and influence juxtaposed and distributed throughout Kuala Lumpur, along with the rich Malay culture and remnants of British colonization. Although the slim majority of Malays practice Islam, they encourage other religious practices. The Batu Caves temple (Hindu) and Chin Swee temple (Buddhist) are beautiful sanctuaries built near the city.

At the Nomad Capitalist event, the keynote speakers included Steve Wozniak, Glenn Greenwald, former UK Chancellor of the Exchequer Kwasi Kwarteng, and MC Andrew Henderson, whose YouTube channel and book I'd studied before signing up. The rest of the speakers talked about how to invest, travel, and diversify away from the United States -- starting businesses and buying property abroad.

I love many things about the US, but as time goes by and as world dynamics shift, my wife and I have taken every opportunity to see the rest of the world. We also have dreams of a second (or first?) home base abroad. Our kids will be grown soon, and it doesn't take a mathematician to see that the cost of living in a place like Malaysia (or even Mexico) grants you a lot more freedom to travel, start a business, or just retire and enjoy family life if you choose.

I missed a lot on this trip. I didn't get to visit the rainforest park or the bird park in the middle of the city. I missed going up to the top of the Petronas Twin Towers and crossing the skybridge -- the one with the glass floor, 558 feet off the ground. I missed going to one of the many bars or restaurants at the top of a skyscraper. And I completely missed the durian, the tropical fruit native to Southeast Asia that you have to wear gloves to eat, and that's so controversial and smelly it's banned from most indoor spaces. I'm told you'll either love it or hate it, but there are very few foods that I hate.

These missed adventures give me reasons to go back. But they're not the real reason I think about Malaysia.

## Post 3: A Bridge Back to Sanity

When I got back home, my mom had major surgery scheduled. I had considered cancelling my trip. I was immediately plunged into spending most of my day confined to a hospital or physical therapy cell, trying and failing to care for my mother when she was not strong enough to recover from surgery. Trying and failing to reason with less-than-reasonable people at work who had zero sympathy for my situation, who expected me to work through a government shutdown while I was watching my mom die, as they were at home playing Dungeons & Dragons, forbidden to work.

I felt almost guilty, at times, for going through with my plans. But I now realize that the freedom and optimism of that trip kept me from being overwhelmed with the stress, sorrow, and loss that I was confined to when I returned.

I started thinking of travel not just as a great adventure, but my own form of therapy. The memories I made were a bridge back to sanity when my life buried me in burdens.

Malaysia made me realize that there's a big world out there to explore, teeming with rich life and culture. That realization didn't make the hospital any quieter or the surgery any less devastating. But it gave me something to hold onto -- a memory of standing in a market eating street food that rivaled anything I'd had at home, for less than the cost of a coffee. A memory of skyscrapers rising from the jungle. A memory of a hotel built in 1932 where $110 bought you a king bed and a standalone bathtub.

I want to take my family back to Malaysia. I want them to taste that watermelon and see those skyscrapers. I want to try the durian. But I also know now that the next trip, wherever it is, will do something else for me. It will give me another bridge. Another set of memories to carry me through whatever comes next.

That's what travel has become. Not an escape. A bridge.
